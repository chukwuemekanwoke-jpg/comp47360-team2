\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Andrew Mitchell, Chukwuemeka Nwoke, Milo Dennehy, Rui Xu, Yang Liu, Yuhao Xu}

\textit{University College Dublin}\\

\begin{abstract}
Urban diners waste time physically checking restaurant availability, and restaurants lose walk-in revenue to unpredictable operational lulls. We propose that this is an issue of information scarcity, or of high consumer search costs \cite{stigler1961economics}, which contribute to demand-supply frictions for dining. To alleviate these demand-supply frictions we developed \textit{Tabl\'{e}}, a two-sided web and mobile dynamic dining marketplace application, that connects diners with immediate table availability. The application curates spatially accessible dining opportunities for consumers, and serves as a marketplace for businesses to advertise tables through a heuristic matching system. Bookings are validated against a transit-aware estimated time of arrival, so a diner cannot hold a table they cannot reach before the hold expires, and merchants track the outcome through a revenue per available seat hour meter. A key element of this application is supply-demand prediction, in the form of restaurant busyness, which we model as a three-class ordinal classification problem. For this prediction task, we trained an XGBoost classifier on 367{,}437 restaurant hourly busyness observations covering 2{,}815 Manhattan restaurants across 60 taxi zones, using New York City Taxi and Limousine Commission drop-off volumes, venue attributes, and cyclical time encodings. Across five model families under a restaurant-disjoint split, gradient-boosted trees performed best, reaching 62.7\% accuracy, 0.624 weighted F1 and 0.599 quadratic weighted kappa on 105{,}937 observations from venues absent from training. Errors concentrate between adjacent classes: fewer than one in ten severely queued observations is labelled no wait. The classifier is served from a FastAPI container behind a Node.js gateway, with scores persisted under a one-hour refresh so discovery reads storage rather than blocking on inference, giving an 82.7\,ms 95th-percentile discovery latency at 30 concurrent users. No incumbent reservation platform we surveyed combines predicted busyness, immediate availability, arrival-time feasibility and private one-to-one lull mitigation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Dining in a dense urban core such as Manhattan involves a persistent information asymmetry. A diner standing on a street corner at 19:00 on a Friday cannot observe, without walking to each venue, which restaurants within reach have a free table or how long the wait is likely to be. A restaurant experiencing an unexpected mid-afternoon lull has no low-friction way to attract nearby demand without public mass discounting, which dilutes brand equity and teaches customers to wait for coupons. There is a need for a solution for reducing these frictions for both restaurants and consumers, to better serve both.

Revenue Management is the process of optimizing profits and customer experiences, through management of price, time, and space, based on customer demand and preferences data. Frequently, this takes the form of dynamic pricing or controlling arrival and duration of customer flows \cite{kimes2004restaurant}. It is employed in industries which possess highly perishable goods or services, high fixed costs, and relatively fixed capacity \cite{kimes2015revenue}.  The restaurant industry is an example of these characteristics. Tables have high perishability and frequent turnover, restaurants have fixed capacity, and costs for labour and space are relatively fixed independent of demand.

As of 2024, yearly global food service expenditure reached € 2.916 trillion, rebounding from the after-effects of COVID-19 \cite{deloitte2025foodservice}.  An empirical case study from \cite{kimes2004sherylrestaurant} found around 34\% of yearly revenue for a surveyed restaurant chain came from weekly peak hours. This example underscores the importance of managing the other 66\% of revenue generation associated with non-peak periods in a trillion euro industry.

We propose a novel RM solution for this market, a two-sided, location-based dining marketplace application, \textit{Tabl\'{e}}. On the consumer side, a mobile application shows the user restaurants within 1.5\,km of their live position, filters based on table availability, and validates every booking attempt against their transit-mode-aware estimated time of arrival. Doing so reduces consumer search frictions while disallowing reservations the user cannot reach within the venue's hold window, in turn providing a measure of prevention against lapsed bookings for restaurants.  This map-centric, time-pressured interface risks excluding mobility-impaired and sensory-sensitive users, so we treat accessibility as a functional requirement: accessible-venue tagging, a manual non-map search path, and an accessibility filter that acts as a hard constraint in offer matching rather than a soft preference.

For restaurant web users, a manager facing a lull can push an exclusive, time-boxed flash deal to the best-suited nearby diners rather than broadcasting a public discount. When the consumer receives an offer, it has a set expiry, and the campaign terminates once the released tables are claimed or elapse. Studies have shown that this method of mobile promotions targeted by time and location significantly increase purchases, with same day offers to nearby consumers performing best \cite{fong2015geo}.

A key component of this application, is a supervised busyness model, used to predict restaurant supply and model future costumer demand. The model predicts an ordinal busyness level from three ingredients: hourly taxi drop-off volumes published by the NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC), venue attributes recovered from public listings, and cyclical encodings of hour and weekday. Its training target is the Google Places popular-times index, a published hourly activity value on a 0--100 scale. That target is a proxy, not observed occupancy, and Section~V-B treats its limitations directly.  Rather, this place busyness is used to determine potential supply based on other restaurant factors, including floor area.

This implementation seeks to address three key objectives established in the RM literature. To accurately and efficiently predict current and future customer demand, to implement a successful method of dynamic pricing with consumer awareness a-priori, and to formalise any change in revenue as a result through established KPIs.

The remainder of this paper reviews related work (Section~II), details the architecture (Section~III), formalises the data pipeline and models (Section~IV), reports the evaluation (Section~V), and concludes with future work (Section~VI).

\section{Literature Review}

RM is an expanding domain, RM literature has seen large growth over the last 20 years, across hospitality and tourism sectors \cite{subying2023bibliometric}. Restaurant Revenue Management (RRM) in specific, has become a more common focus in the recent literature, after increased focus on dining data collected during the COVID-19 pandemic \cite{bujalance2025restaurant}. Coupled with recent improvements in Machine Learning (ML) technology, RRM has been the subject of methodological iteration towards more advanced ML applications of demand prediction, such as Neural Networks \cite{choi2022review}. Frequently these approaches benefit from increased consumer, and general, data collection and processing capabilities. The body of literature shows how disparate features are used for future forecasting of customer demand, including weather information, seasonality, other temporal features, and location \cite{liang2023poisson}\cite{ma2018predicting}\cite{chae2024value}. Ma \textit{et al.} \cite{ma2018predicting} evidenced how this big data can be coupled with supervised ML techniques, XGBoost, KNN and Random Forests, to achieve reasonable performance in predicting future customer demand. Notably, this work presents how lightweight supervised approaches are of increased importance to industry practitioners, compared to the restrictive compute required for DL methods. Other more recent work by Liang \textit{et al.} \cite{liang2023poisson} highlights how the customer demand prediction task closely relates to existing short-term demand forecasting developments in the field of urban transportation.

The urban computing literature establishes that aggregate mobility traces are reliable proxies for localised human activity. Zheng \textit{et al.}~\cite{zheng2014urban} frame urban computing as the acquisition, integration and analysis of heterogeneous city data, and identify taxi trajectories as an important activity sensor. Cranshaw \textit{et al.}~\cite{cranshaw2012livehoods} showed that geotagged activity traces reveal the lived dynamics of neighbourhoods, diverging from administrative boundaries. Zhang \textit{et al.}~\cite{zhang2017deep} showed that citywide crowd flows exhibit learnable spatiotemporal regularities, and Toole \textit{et al.}~\cite{toole2015path} estimated travel demand at city scale from opportunistically collected data. Donovan and Work~\cite{donovan2017empiically} validated New York TLC records as a longitudinal instrument for measuring city-scale dynamics. Kitchin~\cite{kitchin2014real} supplies the critical framing: real-time civic data enables responsive urban services, but systems built on it must stay transparent about provenance and uncertainty.

Beyond optimizing future customer demand prediction, RRM has a number of other determinants and blockers. Webb \textit{et al.} \cite{webb2023variable} introduce customer discrimination, to segment and prioritise high paying customers, and an inability to communicate changes in price to customers in advance as open problems in RRM.  Additionally, the development of key performance indicators (KPI) is an important element of RRM,  Kimes~\cite{kimes2004restaurant} formalised the first RRM KPI as revenue per available seat hour (RevPASH) and showed that actively managing off-peak capacity produces measurable gains.

Despite the evidence in the literature that RRM is important for increasing restaurant profitability, commercial platforms fail to cater to the requirements of RRM. A survey of existing incumbents in the restaurant industry (OpenTable, Resy, Tock, Eat App, Yelp Reservations, TableIn, Eveve, TableAgent and TheFork), found all incumbents where architectured around planned reservations, host-stand operations or prepaid event ticketing. OpenTable has the dominant discovery network but no walk-in busyness prediction or device to communicate price changes which successful RRM is highly dependent on. Resy offers flexible inventory release at \$249--\$899 per month, which is out of reach for small independents.  Tock's prepayment engine targets events rather than spontaneous local demand.

Existing players do not address the open problems evidenced within the RRM literature, of forecasting immediate customer demand, or of offering a way to communicate changes in price with customers a-priori and reflective of the pershability reflective of restaurant service. Perhaps the best implementation of these issues, TooGoodToGo, reflects changes in price a-priori for pershable restaurant goods but not in-service which is the primary concern of RRM. Our implementation proposes a solution for both of these areas of address, by integrating customer demand prediction into both sides of our application interface, and by allowing restaurant managers to directly control and notify customers of price changes prior to confirmation of bookings.

\section{Methodology}
The application methodology is described in chronological order, from the project design to final deployment. The sections are as follows, Project Design \& Requirements, System Overview \& Organisation, Data Layer, Backend Service, Frontend, Testing, and Integration \& Deployment.

\subsection{Project Design \& Requirements Elicitation}
Product/UX work framed a two-sided problem: diners cannot judge whether a free table is still reachable, while restaurants lose revenue when short periods of spare capacity pass unused. Three exploratory interviews with student diners tested whether this value proposition was understandable. Since no restaurant operator participated, the evidence was treated as formative rather than market validation. Findings became user stories and acceptance criteria, prioritised into MVP and later-stage features and revised after technical feasibility reviews.

The MVP formed a closed loop: mobile users discover available tables within 1.5\,km, check ETA and reachability, then book or claim an offer; merchants monitor tables and issue private, quota-limited flash deals from the web dashboard. The radius reduces uncertain journeys without hiding nearby choice, while ETA validation addresses the risk that a table disappears before arrival. Private rather than public discounting was selected as a product hypothesis for monetising perishable capacity without discounting to every customer, consistent with restaurant revenue-management principles~\cite{kimes2004restaurant}. Week~5 feedback led us to separate consumer tasks onto mobile and merchant operations onto web. A shared UI guide then standardised typography, colour tokens and semantic states across both clients.

\subsection{System Overview \& Repository Organisation}
\label{sec:system-overview}

Tabl\'{e} is organised as a monorepo with four top-level components: \texttt{frontend/} (web and mobile clients plus shared packages), \texttt{backend/api-gateway/} (the transactional service layer), \texttt{ml-pipeline/} (notebooks and the FastAPI inference service), and \texttt{database/} (versioned migrations, seeds and a local \texttt{docker-compose} harness). The structure lets a six-developer team define JSON API contracts once and evolve all consumers in a single review stream. Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the deployed architecture.

\subsection{Backend Service Layer}
\label{sec:backend}

The transactional core is a Node.js/Express API gateway exposing versioned
REST endpoints to the mobile and merchant clients. Node.js was selected
because this layer primarily performs I/O-bound orchestration across
PostgreSQL, the inference service and external routing APIs. Booking,
campaign, routing and inference responsibilities are separated into service
modules, keeping transactional logic independent of the model implementation.

Model inference is deployed as a separate Python FastAPI service. This
boundary allows the application to use the scientific Python ecosystem while
keeping the model independently deployable behind a validated HTTP
contract~\cite{dragoni2017microservices}. The model pipeline is loaded at
service startup and supports both venue-busyness prediction and flash-deal
candidate ranking.

Booking and campaign writes execute inside PostgreSQL transactions. Relevant
inventory rows are locked before availability is modified, while database
constraints and idempotent writes prevent concurrent or duplicate claims.
Authentication uses password hashing and signed JWTs, with additional
ownership checks on merchant operations and rate limiting on sensitive
endpoints.

External dependencies use bounded fallbacks. ETA calculation falls back to a
local distance estimate when the routing service is unavailable, while
matching falls back to deterministic distance ordering if inference fails.
Previously stored busyness scores remain available during inference outages,
preventing temporary dependency failures from interrupting booking and
discovery workflows.

\subsection{Data Layer and Busyness Persistence}
\label{sec:busyness-persistence}

Application state is stored in PostgreSQL on Cloud SQL and evolved through
versioned migrations. Database constraints enforce booking and campaign
invariants independently of application-level validation, while optional
geospatial support provides a path for moving distance filtering into the
database if future scale requires it.

Busyness scores are persisted with independently tracked prediction freshness.
Discovery requests read the stored scores immediately and trigger bounded,
asynchronous refreshes when they exceed a one-hour TTL. Concurrent refreshes
and repeated failures are suppressed, while the last successful score remains
available if the inference service is unavailable. Consequently, discovery
latency does not depend directly on model latency, as reflected in the
service-performance results in Section~\ref{sec:service-performance}.

A newly onboarded venue has no booking history, so its score comes entirely
from the classifier, which needs only coordinates, venue attributes and the
hour. As bookings accumulate, the service blends the venue's own observed
occupancy into that prediction. Writing $d$ for days since onboarding and $n$
for confirmed bookings in the trailing 30 days, the weight given to observed
occupancy is
\begin{equation}
w = w_{\max}\,\min\!\left(1,\tfrac{d}{30}\right)\frac{n}{n + n_0},
\end{equation}
with $w_{\max} = 0.6$ and pseudo-count $n_0 = 20$, and the served score is
$(1-w)\,\hat{y} + w\,o$ for classifier output $\hat{y}$ and observed occupancy
$o$. Occupancy is measured per (weekday, hour) bucket as bookings landing in
that bucket per weekday occurrence per table, clipped to $[0,1]$. The linear
term ramps over a 30-day maturation window, while the shrinkage term damps it
by evidence volume, so a month-old venue with two bookings does not swing onto
its own noisy history. Capping $w$ at 0.6 leaves the model at least 40\% of the
weight permanently, and the blend is skipped entirely when the gateway supplies
no booking counts, which keeps the cold-start path identical to the
classifier's own output.

\subsection{Frontend}

\subsubsection{Tools \& Frameworks}
The web client uses React and Vite. We chose it over heavier server-rendered frameworks because the core surfaces, a live map, a discovery list, a booking flow and a merchant dashboard, are interaction-dense per-user views that gain little from server-side rendering, while Vite's sub-second hot-module replacement mattered for quick development in a compressed five-sprint schedule. The mobile client is React Native under Expo, a JavaScript framework chosen so team members and service-wrapper layer can serve both platforms without native build-chain maintenance. Mobile development alternatives such as Kotlin or Swift were not considered as we sought a cross platform (CP) language to support CP deployment; additionally, popular alternative Flutter was not chosen despite its advantages in CP consistency, due to pixel based rendering rather than native components, because of the development requirements associated with different mobile and web languages. Both clients rely on shared single typed API contract and state store, built using Redux Toolkit Query, that centralises every network call, its authentication headers and its cache-invalidation rules. This contract is unit-tested against a mocked \texttt{fetch}, so every call site is exercised in continuous integration rather than diverging silently between platforms. The merchant dashboard additionally draws on a shared \texttt{table-*} design-token system for visual consistency with the rest of the product.

\subsubsection{Design}

The consumer-facing mobile client is the primary surface by which the application seeks to address consumer-sided search costs. This objective informs a number of design decisions, such as discovery views, map extent filtering, and search filtering. Technically, the mobile application is organised as a series of core views or "tabs", which reuse modular React Native components. Utilising the Expo framework router a set of web specific views are also provided using the .web file suffix, this guarantees web support of the mobile application by removing Native specific functionality for web alternatives. The mobile application relies extensively on the operations of the shared RTK store and slice for fetching, refreshing, and caching both client-side generated state and server-side state. This enables the application design, which defines a number of views or tabs remain stateful during navigation between tabs. An example of this with client-side state, is the search and filter functionality which apply to restaurants across the Card and Map views. In a data intensive customer facing application this state retention is required to improve usability and is explicitly specified as a requirement during the project design phase.

The merchant-facing web client is the specific surface through which a restaurant manager perceives and acts on the signals described in Sections~\ref{sec:busyness-persistence} and IV-D: predicted busyness, live occupancy, and the flash-deal mechanism used for lull mitigation. The authenticated session is held in a React context provider, while per-restaurant data (identity, live occupancy, capacity) is resolved once in a shared layout route and passed down through React Router's outlet context, so individual dashboard views consume it without re-fetching or re-declaring it themselves. Alongside mobile, it makes use of the RTK contract to centralise cache invalidation, for example: creating or cancelling a campaign, refreshes both the active-campaign status shown on the overview page and the campaign history list, without either view needing to know the other exists. To satisfy our objective of implementing an RRM KPI, the dashboard presents a RevPASH meter implementing Kimes's metric~\cite{kimes2004restaurant} alongside the reservation list and the campaign-creation form, so a manager can observe, decide and act inside one view.

\subsubsection{Accessibility and Inclusion}
\emph{Accessibility} is treated as a functional constraint rather than a styling concern, consistent with the posture argued in Section~I: mobile customers and web managers both flag wheelchair and sensory-friendly accommodation and requirements during onboarding, this feeds directly into the hard accessibility veto applied during flash-deal matching (Section~IV-D). Both interfaces provide a light/dark theme, ARIA live regions on state that updates without user action, and web provides a layout that collapses the primary navigation to a top bar below the tablet breakpoint rather than hiding functionality on small screens. Each of these decisions was traced to a specific project persona or formal user story rather than to internal preference; Section~V-C audits this mapping directly, including the decisions made specifically for the project's EDI-focused persona.

\subsection{Testing}
\subsubsection{Unit \& Integration Testing}
The web client carries 44 automated tests across 7 suites (Vitest and Testing Library); five of the seven mock the \texttt{apiSlice} contract directly to test authentication and onboarding flows in isolation, while the remaining two cover form-input behaviour and pure validation logic. The suite is run, alongside linting and a production build, as a required check on every pull request. Relative to the analytics and service layers described elsewhere in this paper, the web client's contribution is narrow in scope but load-bearing in function: it is the point at which the busyness model, the RevPASH metric and the matching heuristic become a decision a person actually makes.

\subsubsection{User Testing}

Successful application development frequently relies on an iterative approach, as evidenced by the leading development methodologies \cite{beck2001manifesto}. This iterative approach requires consistent user testing to inform application design direction. There a number of disparate usability testing methodologies: from interviews, to questionairres, to think-aloud processes \cite{weichbroth2020usability}.  To improve on the application iteratively in a short period of development time, the Rapid Iterative Testing and Evaluation (RITE) \cite{medlock2002using} method was chosen as the usability framework used during development. This testing framework prioritises rapid changes, often between usability participants or frequently occuring rounds. During this testing we predominantly rely on the Concurrently Think-aloud (CTA) \cite{ericsson1984protocol} method of elicitation, where participants verbalise their thought process while engaging in a number of key set tasks with the application. This unstructured elicitation reduces the testing time for both participants and moderators, supporting the rapid iteration of RITE, while still proving accurate \cite{cooke2010assessing}.

Formative evaluation combined an internal workflow review with four moderated, task-based sessions: one Product/UX evaluator (P01) and three external student peers (P02--P04). Participants used both clients to discover and book a restaurant, claim an offer, create a merchant campaign and inspect its status while thinking aloud~\cite{cooke2010assessing}. Notes and task outcomes were anonymised and consolidated into a prioritised change list. The internal review had already exposed the lack of a browsing route for users without a chosen restaurant, leading to the Discover tab. Completion time and standardised usability scores were not collected. An additional qualitative user-testing round on the mobile client with two external students produced a prioritised list of functionality gaps, each recorded with its rationale and resolution in a tracked change log.

\subsection{Deployment, Delivery and Operational Governance}

All services are containerized and deployed to Google Cloud Run, chosen over self-managed Kubernetes because scale-to-zero containers remove idle cost and operational burden for our team while preserving a migration path to GKE Autopilot. The web client is served through Firebase Hosting, deployed from CI (\texttt{deploy-staging.yml}). Delivery follows a three-tier branch promotion model: feature branches merge into \texttt{integrate}, which promotes to \texttt{develop} (staging) and then to \texttt{main} (production), in every case by pull request, so the demo environment cannot receive unreviewed code. Operational risk is tracked in a version-controlled risk register and a rollback-and-recovery runbook covering database down-migrations, Cloud Run revision pinning, and Firebase Hosting release rollback. The repository additionally carries Terraform describing the staging estate, together with equivalent AWS and Azure designs, so the deployment is reproducible rather than click-configured. External integrations are the Google Routes API for ETA computation (cached, to respect a student budget), Firebase Cloud Messaging for offer delivery, and a simulated Stripe integration for deposit flows.

All measurements in Section~V were taken against the staging deployment described above. That environment was decommissioned after the evaluation campaign, so the figures reported here are historical measurements rather than claims about a currently reachable service.

\section{Data Analytics and Visualization}

\subsection{Datasets}

Table's analytical layer is built exclusively on open data. Four primary sources are ingested by the notebook pipeline (\texttt{data\_engineering\_v1/v2}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{NYC TLC taxi trip records}, aggregated into \texttt{manhattan\_hourly\_demand.csv}: mean drop-off counts per (taxi zone, weekday, hour) cell, serving as the primary area-activity proxy.
\item \textbf{NYC taxi-zone shapefile} (\texttt{taxi\_zones.zip}), the official TLC polygon geometry used for spatial assignment.
\item \textbf{NYC PLUTO and restaurant metadata} (\texttt{manhattan\_pluto.csv}, \texttt{manhattan\_restaurants.csv}): land-use context and the cleaned restaurant universe, including real neighbourhood, phone, and accessibility attributes that replaced earlier simulated fields.
\end{itemize}

Supplementary derived tables (cuisine profiles, restaurant supply, popular-times joins against June taxi demand) support the recommendation notebook, and a simulated cohort (\texttt{sim\_users}, \texttt{sim\_orders}, \texttt{sim\_promotions}) provides demo-safe marketplace activity where real transaction data cannot exist for an academic prototype. Commercial mobile-location foot-traffic data was deliberately excluded: it is not available as open data and would require a paid vendor licence, which conflicts with both the project budget and its open-data commitment.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Feature Engineering}

This study predicts restaurant waiting conditions rather than exact waiting times. Google Maps busyness scores range from 0 to 100, with zero indicating immediate availability and positive values representing varying congestion levels (Figure~\ref{fig:busyness_score_distribution}). Minor score differences provide limited practical value to diners. The continuous score was therefore converted into three ordinal classes: \textit{No Wait}, \textit{Queue Required}, and \textit{Severe Queue}. Three classes preserve the distinction between moderate and severe congestion while maintaining practical interpretability and manageable predictive complexity.

Review count was log-transformed because of its highly skewed distribution (skewness = 13.594), reducing the influence of extreme values. Restaurant floor area was estimated using commercial-area allocation rules, while cuisine-specific turnover and takeaway ratios were derived from industry evidence. Detailed descriptive statistics and feature-processing methods are reported in Tables~\ref{tab:descriptive_statistics} and~\ref{tab:feature_engineering}.

\subsection{Model Selection}

Restaurant busyness was formulated as an ordinal classification problem with three waiting levels. Logistic Regression served as the primary baseline because of its simplicity and interpretability, while a single Decision Tree provided an interpretable nonlinear benchmark. KNN was included to test whether restaurants with similar operational and demand characteristics exhibit similar waiting conditions. Random Forest was selected to reduce the overfitting and instability of a single tree through bootstrap aggregation and feature randomisation. XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} was included for its strong performance on structured tabular data and its ability to capture complex interactions among temporal, spatial, demand, and capacity features. The comparison evaluates whether increasingly flexible nonlinear and ensemble models improve predictive performance over the baseline.

\subsection{Flash-Deal Candidate Matching}

When a restaurant triggers a lull-mitigation campaign, the gateway pre-filters eligible diners (opted-in, within range, preference-compatible) and the ML service ranks them by the interpretable baseline heuristic
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(u) = 0.6\max\!\left(0,\,1-\tfrac{d_u}{1500}\right) + 0.2\,\mathbb{1}[\text{budget}_u] + 0.1\,\mathbb{1}[\text{diet}_u] + \varepsilon,
\end{equation}
with $d_u$ the diner's distance in metres, indicator terms rewarding completed preference profiles, and $\varepsilon \sim U(0, 0.05)$ a small tie-breaking jitter that prevents deterministic starvation among near-identical candidates. The 1\,500\,m normaliser mirrors the discovery radius, so the distance term vanishes exactly at the edge of the reachable range. Consistent with the risk posture recorded in the project plan, this transparent baseline precedes any learned model: it is auditable, degrades predictably, and can be replaced behind the stable \texttt{/api/v1/match} contract once labelled acceptance data accumulates.

\subsection{Visual Insights}

Figure~\ref{fig:Busyness Category Composition Across Review, Rating and Duration} shows the distribution of busyness categories across different restaurant characteristics. Review volume exhibits little association with busyness, as the proportions of the three categories remain relatively stable across all review groups. In contrast, higher-rated restaurants display a larger proportion of No Wait observations and a lower proportion of Severe Queue, suggesting that operational quality may mitigate congestion. Similarly, restaurants with longer typical visit durations tend to experience fewer severe queues and more no-wait periods, indicating that visit duration alone does not necessarily increase waiting times and may instead reflect greater seating capacity or more efficient operations.

Figure~\ref{fig:Busyness Category Composition Across Area, Turnover and Takeaway} shows the relationship between operational restaurant characteristics and busyness categories. Restaurant area exhibits only a weak association with congestion, with slightly higher proportions of severe queues among larger restaurants. In contrast, turnover rate shows a clearer trend, as higher turnover groups contain fewer No Wait observations and more Severe Queue observations, suggesting greater operational intensity during busy periods. A similar pattern is observed for takeaway ratio, where restaurants with higher takeaway activity tend to experience more severe congestion. These findings indicate that operational characteristics provide useful information for predicting restaurant busyness.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This paper presented Tabl\'{e}, a two-sided marketplace and analytics framework that reframes urban dining from a planned-reservation problem into a real-time, spatially validated matching problem. Its engineering contribution is a separated microservice architecture, a JavaScript transaction layer, a Python analytics layer and a versioned relational core, deployable by a small team on serverless infrastructure with auditable promotion and rollback governance. Its analytical contribution is a supervised busyness classifier trained on open taxi records and public venue attributes, validated on venues held out entirely from training, reaching 62.7\% accuracy and 0.599 quadratic weighted kappa on a three-level ordinal target, with neither extreme error direction exceeding one in ten observations within its class. Its product contribution is the combination, absent from all nine incumbents surveyed, of predicted busyness, immediate availability, ETA feasibility guardrails and private one-to-one lull mitigation.

Four directions follow. The most valuable is \emph{ground truth}: every model figure here is measured against a proxy label, and instrumenting real venues with observed wait times, even a small panel, would convert this from a study of published activity indices into a study of queues. Second, \emph{streaming ingestion}: the pipeline currently ships precomputed aggregates with the inference container, and moving to a streaming architecture would let predictions track same-day anomalies such as weather, events and transit disruption rather than historical norms. Third, \emph{learned matching}: the flash-deal heuristic is transparent but unvalidated, and once accepted offers accumulate it can be replaced behind its existing contract, retaining the accessibility veto as a hard constraint rather than a learned weight. Fourth, \emph{spatial generalisation}: the model's only Manhattan-specific dependencies are the taxi-zone geometry and the venue attribute source, so porting it to any city publishing comparable open mobility data would test whether open civic data alone can sustain this class of service.

Alongside these technical directions, product validation should extend beyond the small student sample to restaurant operators and more diverse diners, using task metrics and a formal accessibility audit to test usability and fit with restaurant practice.
